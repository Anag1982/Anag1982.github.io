Attention, Derived · Issue 3

Why attention divides by √d

18 September 2026·6 min read·Avishek Nag

Last issue the stream of embedded vectors arrived at the mechanism this series is named for, and the formula carries a curious constant: the attention score is divided by √dh before anything else happens. It is the most-quoted and least-examined constant in the architecture. It has a real justification, the justification has two hypotheses, and both hypotheses are false by the end of the first training step. All three of those statements matter, and this issue derives every one of them from a blank page.

The score, and the constant in it

Attention compares a query vector q against every key vector k by an inner product, turns the scores into weights with a softmax, and averages the value vectors with those weights. Written with the projections in place:

Attn(X) = softmax( Q Kᵀ / √dₕ ) V

Every symbol in that line is forced by a requirement except the √dh, where dh is the width of the head. The folklore explanation says “we divide because the dot product grows with dimension,” and it is worth being precise about why that sentence, as usually meant, is wrong. The dot product of two independent random vectors does not grow deterministically. Its mean is zero. What grows is its spread, and the spread is what the derivation is actually about.

The variance argument, from nothing

Take q and k in dh dimensions, independent of each other, entries independent with mean zero and variance one. The score is the sum of dh products qᵢkᵢ. Each product has mean zero, because the factors are independent. For the variance, square the sum and take expectations: the cross terms die, again by independence, and each of the dh diagonal terms contributes exactly 1. So the score has mean 0 and variance dh, which makes its standard deviation √dh, and dividing by √dh restores unit variance at any head width. That is the whole derivation, and a simulation agrees to two decimal places:

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

for dh in (16, 128, 1024):
    q = rng.normal(size=(100_000, dh))
    k = rng.normal(size=(100_000, dh))
    z = (q * k).sum(axis=1)
    print(dh, z.var().round(1), (z / np.sqrt(dh)).var().round(2))
# 16    16.0   1.0
# 128  126.7   0.99
# 1024 1021.9  1.0

Now the magnitude check that gives the constant its teeth. At dh = 128, unscaled logits have standard deviation √128 ≈ 11.3, so a two-standard-deviation gap between the top two scores is over 20. On its own a big score is harmless. The harm arrives through the softmax, and it arrives as a dead gradient.

What a big gap does to the gradient

The softmax’s Jacobian, the matrix of how every output weight responds to every input score, is diag(p) − p pᵗ. Two of its properties do the damage. It kills the all-ones direction, because adding the same constant to every score changes nothing, which is harmless. And its largest singular value is bounded by 4(n − 1)e−Δ, where Δ is the gap between the largest and second-largest score. The gradient reaching the query and key weights dies exponentially in the gap.

Put the two halves together and the constant explains itself. Unscaled, the gap between the top two logits grows like √dh. The gradient dies like e to the minus gap. The division exists to stop the exponent being of order √dh, and a head whose top two logits differ by 15 has ‖J‖₂ below 10⁻⁵: that head is not learning slowly, it has stopped. The argument is about gradient flow. Numerical overflow is a real concern and a separate one, handled by max-subtraction in every implementation, and conflating the two is how the folklore version went wrong.

Both hypotheses are false, and the factor survives

Now collect the assumptions the derivation spent: entries of mean zero and variance one, independent within each vector, and q independent of k. None of them is enforced by the architecture. All of them describe initialisation, and training breaks them immediately, because the optimiser is under no obligation to keep entry variances at one. The independence of q and k is worse than false: it is structurally wrong even at step zero, since q and k are two linear images of the same residual vector.

The failure mode this produces is well documented. Entry variance drifts up during training, logit variance grows as its square times dh, the softmax saturates, and the Jacobian bound collapses. The symptom is attention-entropy collapse: the loss plateaus with one head’s maximum weight pinned at 1.000, and divergence follows thousands of steps later. The current fix stops assuming and starts enforcing. QK-norm normalises q and k before the inner product, so the logit is √dh cos θ and bounded by construction. The irony is worth stating plainly: QK-norm makes the scaling factor’s original justification obsolete while leaving the factor itself correct. √dh is a constant that is right by luck rather than by guarantee, and now you know exactly which luck.

Next issue faces the fact the whole derivation quietly used: attention treats its inputs as an unordered set. Nothing in the score knows that dog bites man differs from man bites dog, so position has to be injected, and the modern way to inject it is strange and lovely: rotate every query and key by an angle proportional to its position, and let the inner product read off the difference. Why rotation, of all things, is the right tool is a derivation of its own.
