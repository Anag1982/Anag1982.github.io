Attention, Derived · Issue 1

Eight billion parameters, counted by hand

21 August 2026·5 min read·Avishek Nag

You can already multiply matrices and differentiate. So why does the first arXiv paper you open about large language models defeat you?

Three reasons, and none of them is mathematics. The paper writes Wx and the code writes x @ W. The paper calls a gradient a Jacobian, and means something specific by it that it does not stop to say. And the paper assumes you know that cross-entropy through a softmax collapses to a subtraction, so it writes p − y without comment and moves on. Each of these is a convention, not a result. Together they cost a competent reader three or four hours per paper, and the hours are spent reconstructing notation rather than learning anything.

This is the first issue of a fortnightly series on the mathematics inside transformers and large language models, and its method is in the name. Each issue takes one result, the attention scaling factor, rotary position embeddings, the KV-cache arithmetic, the Adam update, states it precisely, derives it in full, and then shows what it costs in practice: memory, compute, or behaviour. Derivation-led, because a result you have derived is a result you can adapt when the architecture shifts under you, and it will shift. The instances in this field have a half-life of a couple of years. The mathematics does not.

To show what that buys you, this opening issue does the simplest derivation in the subject, and one of the most useful. We count a model.

A model is defined by seven numbers

Take a transformer I will call Model D, defined entirely by its hyperparameters: 32 layers, model width d = 4096, 32 attention heads of dimension 128, grouped-query attention with 8 key-value heads, a feed-forward width of 14336 with SwiGLU gating, and a vocabulary of 128,256 tokens. If those numbers look familiar from a popular open-weights model, that is not an accident, but nothing below depends on whose model it is. The seven numbers are sufficient.

Each layer holds four attention matrices. The query projection is d × (heads × head dim) = 4096 × 4096. The key and value projections are smaller, because grouped-query attention gives them only 8 heads each: 4096 × 1024. The output projection mirrors the query. The feed-forward block holds three matrices of size 4096 × 14336, gate, up and down. And each layer carries two RMSNorm gain vectors of length 4096, which is all the normalisation a modern decoder needs.

d, h, d_h, n_kv = 4096, 32, 128, 8
d_ff, L, V = 14336, 32, 128256

attn  = d*h*d_h + 2*d*n_kv*d_h + h*d_h*d   # Q, K and V, O projections
mlp   = 3 * d * d_ff                       # SwiGLU: gate, up, down
layer = attn + mlp + 2*d                   # plus two RMSNorm gains

total = L*layer + d + 2*V*d                # final norm, embed, unembed
print(layer)              # 218112000
print(total)              # 8030261248
print(total * 2 / 1e9)    # 16.060522496  bytes in bf16, as GB

Run it and the count lands on 8,030,261,248. An eight-billion-parameter model, counted end to end on the back of a boarding pass, with nothing assumed beyond multiplication.

Two readings of the count

The count is not the point. What it makes visible is.

First reading: the feed-forward block is 81 percent of each layer, 176.2 million of its 218.1 million parameters. The component the whole field is named after, attention, is a minority shareholder in its own architecture. When later issues derive what the feed-forward block actually does, key-value memory, not mere plumbing, you will already know why it deserves the space.

Second reading: grouped-query attention is visible in the arithmetic before anyone mentions inference. With a full 32 key-value heads, the four attention matrices would cost 67.1 million per layer; at 8 they cost 41.9 million. The choice removed 0.81 billion parameters from the model, and the real reason for it, the KV cache, gets its own issue.

And one practical consequence, free of charge. Bytes are count times precision, so Model D occupies 16.06 GB in bf16 and will not fit on a 16 GB card. The answer to “will this fit” is arithmetic, not experiment. That sentence is the spirit of the whole series.

Who this is for

It is written for the engineer or student who can use these models and is tired of not knowing what is inside them: the person who has read that attention scores are divided by √d and never seen anyone derive why, or who nods along to “the KV cache is the bottleneck” without being able to compute the cache’s size. Undergraduate linear algebra, calculus and probability are assumed. Nothing else is. Every claim will be derived rather than asserted, every number will come from arithmetic you can rerun, and where a topic is moving fast I will say so and date the claim, because pretending otherwise is how technical writing goes quietly wrong.

Next issue the derivations start in earnest, at the front door of the model: the embedding matrix. Why a token lookup is secretly a matrix product, what cosine similarity actually measures, and the strange, provable fact that in 4,096 dimensions almost every pair of random vectors is nearly perpendicular, which is the property the whole enterprise quietly rests on.
