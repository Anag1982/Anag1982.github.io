Amplitudes · Issue 6

The three families that run everything

30 October 2026·6 min read·Avishek Nag

Issue 4 established that a gate is a matrix, and issue 5 built the inner product that compares two states. This issue sorts the matrices themselves. Out of all the matrices that exist, quantum computing runs on exactly three special families. Hermitian matrices hold the numbers a measurement can report. Unitary matrices hold every operation a quantum computer is allowed to perform. Projectors do the reporting, picking one outcome out of a state. Each family is defined by one short equation, each equation has a one-line NumPy test, and each earns its place for a reason you can prove in a few lines. Learn the three and the rest of the subject is vocabulary.

Hermitian: equal to its own dagger

The dagger, from issue 5, is conjugate-transpose: flip the matrix across its diagonal and conjugate every entry. A matrix equal to its own dagger, A = A†, is called Hermitian. The most famous example in the subject is the Pauli Y matrix, whose entries are purely imaginary and yet whose dagger lands exactly back on itself.

import numpy as np

Y = np.array([[0, -1j],
              [1j,  0]])

print(np.allclose(Y, Y.conj().T))   # True -> Y is Hermitian
print(np.linalg.eigvalsh(Y))        # [-1.  1.] -> both eigenvalues real

Those eigenvalues being real is not a coincidence of this example. It is the family’s defining theorem, and the proof is two lines with issue 5’s machinery. Take an eigenvector v of a Hermitian A with eigenvalue λ, and look at the single number v†Av. Dagger the whole expression and it comes back to itself, because (v†Av)† = v†A†v = v†Av, so the number equals its own conjugate, which forces it to be real. But v†Av is also λ times v†v, and v†v is a real positive length. A real number divided by a real number: λ is real. Every eigenvalue of every Hermitian matrix, whatever imaginary soup its entries are, is a real number. That is why physics stores every measurable quantity, energy, spin, position, in a Hermitian matrix: the eigenvalues are the values a measurement can return, and a laboratory instrument cannot display 3 + 2i.

Unitary: the length-preservers

The second family was introduced in issue 4 as the rule a gate must obey; now it earns its place. A matrix U is unitary when U†U = I, that is, when its dagger is its own inverse. The consequence that matters sits one line away. Send any state v through U and compute the new length-squared: (Uv)†(Uv) = v†U†Uv = v†v. The length does not change. Ever, for any input. And length is not decoration here: issue 3 established that the squared amplitudes of a state are probabilities and must total 1. A matrix that changed a state’s length would create or destroy probability, and so could not be a legal operation on a qubit. Thus every gate must be unitary, and every unitary is a legal gate. The rule also hands you reversibility for free: U† undoes U exactly, so a quantum computation can always be run backwards. A classical AND gate, which maps two bits to one and forgets its inputs, has no such inverse, and that difference in reversibility is one of the deepest dividing lines between the two kinds of computing.

H = np.array([[1,  1],
              [1, -1]]) / np.sqrt(2)

print(np.allclose(H.conj().T @ H, np.eye(2)))   # True -> H is unitary

v = np.array([0.6, 0.8])
print(np.linalg.norm(v))       # 1.0
print(np.linalg.norm(H @ v))   # 0.9999999999999999 -> the length survives

Note that H passes the Hermitian test too: it equals its own dagger. A matrix can hold dual citizenship, and the Hadamard gate is the celebrated case, both a legal gate and a measurable quantity, its own inverse into the bargain. The families overlap; the membership tests do not blur.

Projectors: the matrices that choose

The third family is the strangest and the most physical. A projector is a Hermitian matrix with P² = P: applying it twice is the same as applying it once. The simplest one is built from a basis state by the outer product of issue 5, P₀ = |0⟩⟨0|, which in matrix form has a single 1 in the top-left corner. Feed it any state and it keeps the |0⟩ part and deletes the rest, casting the state’s shadow onto one axis. The equation P² = P is just the geometry talking: once a vector is flat on the floor, flattening it again changes nothing.

ket0 = np.array([[1.0],
                 [0.0]])
P0 = ket0 @ ket0.conj().T

print(np.allclose(P0 @ P0, P0))   # True -> a projector: twice is once

psi = np.array([[0.6],
                [0.8]])
prob = (psi.conj().T @ P0 @ psi).real.item()
print(prob)                       # 0.36 -> the chance a measurement finds 0

That last number closes the loop with issue 5. For the state ψ = 0.6|0⟩ + 0.8|1⟩, the quantity ⟨ψ|P₀|ψ⟩ works out to 0.36, which is exactly |⟨0|ψ⟩|², the squared overlap that issue 5 named as the probability of finding the state as |0⟩. A measurement, in the mathematics, is a family of projectors, one per possible outcome, and the state’s squared shadow on each one is that outcome’s probability. The projector does not have to point along a basis axis either: build P₊ = |+⟩⟨+| from the diagonal state and every entry is 0.5, and it asks a different question of the same state.

One honest caution: the three tests are numerical, and np.allclose is doing quiet work in every one of them. Floating-point arithmetic leaves residues of 10⁻¹⁶ in matrices that are exactly Hermitian or unitary on paper, so an equality test written with == will betray you where a tolerance test will not. The companion notebook wraps all three tests into predicate functions, is_hermitian, is_unitary, is_projector, and runs them across a zoo of examples, including the matrices that fool a first guess: diag(1, i) is unitary but not Hermitian, diag(2, 3) is Hermitian but nothing else, and X, like H, is both.

You can now write states, move them with unitaries, and ask projectors for the probability of each outcome. What this issue did not explain is where the measurement results themselves live: the real eigenvalues of the Hermitian section were asserted to be “the numbers a measurement can report” without saying which number goes with which state. Next issue makes that exact. The eigen-equation Av = λv sorts a matrix into its own private directions and the numbers attached to them, diagonalisation rewrites the matrix as nothing but those numbers, and the punchline of the whole subject lands: the eigenvalues are the measurement results, and the eigenvectors are the states the measurement leaves behind.
