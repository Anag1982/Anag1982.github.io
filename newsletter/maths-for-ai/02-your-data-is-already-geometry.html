The Maths Underneath · Issue 2

Your data is already geometry

21 August 2026·7 min read·Avishek Nag

A set of GPS coordinates is a list of two numbers that means nothing when you read the numbers separately. So is the row of slider positions on an audio equaliser, and the line of figures at the bottom of a receipt. In each case the numbers only carry meaning together, as a group, in a fixed order.

You have been using vectors for years. This issue gives the habit its name, and then shows what you get in return, which is more than a piece of terminology.

A row of data is a point on a map

Streaming services describe every track with numbers between 0 and 1. How energetic it is, how danceable, how acoustic. Take the first two for one upbeat song:

s = [0.82, 0.65]
     energy  danceability

On its own, 0.82 is a number. Paired with the second value it stops being a number and becomes a location. Draw an axis for energy and an axis for danceability, and that pair fixes the song to one spot on a map of all possible songs.

That is the whole idea of a vector. A list of numbers becomes a position in space.

In NumPy there is nothing to build. The row is already the object:

import numpy as np

song = np.array([0.82, 0.65])   # [energy, danceability]

print(song)          # [0.82 0.65]
print(song.shape)    # (2,)   a 1-D array with 2 components
print(song[0])       # 0.82   the energy component

Stop picturing a spreadsheet and picture a map. Every row of your dataset is a pin dropped on it. Rows that land near each other are similar things. Everything in this series follows from taking that sentence literally.

Two components let us draw the picture. Nothing breaks when there are more. A photograph handed to a vision model becomes roughly a thousand numbers, and a word inside a large language model becomes around 768. You lose the ability to sketch it, and you keep every piece of mathematics that follows, unchanged.

The same numbers, read two ways

It is worth separating two readings of the same vector, because both are used constantly and switching between them freely is most of what intuition means here.

Take a delivery drone and the vector [3, 2]. Read as a point, it is a location: the drone sits 3 km east and 2 km north of its base. Read as an arrow, the same two numbers are a movement: travel 3 km east and 2 km north, starting from wherever you happen to be.

Both readings are correct and you pick whichever helps. A location is natural when the vector stands for a thing, a song or a customer. An arrow is natural when it stands for a change, such as the small correction a model makes to itself during one step of training. When we reach gradient descent, that second reading is the one that matters.

Distance is difference

Here is the return on treating data as geometry. If every item is a point, then the distance between two points measures how unalike the two items are. Similarity stops being a vague idea and becomes a number you can compute.

To get it, subtract one vector from the other to obtain the arrow between them, then measure that arrow's length. Length comes from Pythagoras, extended to as many components as you have:

dist(a, b) = sqrt( (a₁ - b₁)² + (a₂ - b₂)² + ... )

Describe each house by [bedrooms, size]. House A is [1, 1.2], small and one-bed. House B is [3.8, 4.0], large and four-bed. One number now summarises how different they are:

house_A = np.array([1.0, 1.2])
house_B = np.array([3.8, 4.0])

print(np.linalg.norm(house_A - house_B))   # 3.96

np.linalg.norm is measuring the length of the arrow between the two points. Small distance means similar homes. Large distance means very different ones.

The part that goes wrong in practice

I should be honest about where this breaks, because it breaks often and quietly.

Distance treats every axis as equally important. It has no idea what your columns mean. Suppose you describe houses as [bedrooms, size in square metres] and use the raw numbers:

a = np.array([1, 45.0])    # 1 bedroom,  45 m²
b = np.array([4, 45.0])    # 4 bedrooms, same size
c = np.array([1, 55.0])    # 1 bedroom,  10 m² bigger

print(np.linalg.norm(a - b))   # 3.0    three extra bedrooms
print(np.linalg.norm(a - c))   # 10.0   ten extra square metres

By this measure, ten square metres of extra floor space makes a house three times more different than three additional bedrooms. That is nonsense, and no model told you it was happening. Square metres run into the hundreds while bedroom counts run in single digits, so the size column quietly decides every comparison on its own.

The fix is to put the columns on comparable scales first, usually by subtracting each column's mean and dividing by its standard deviation. On a typical housing dataset, where bedroom counts vary by about 1.5 and floor areas by about 30 m², the same two comparisons on standardised data give 2.01 for the three extra bedrooms against 0.34 for the ten extra square metres. The bedrooms now dominate, which is what a person would say if you asked them.

Note that this is not a detail of implementation. Distance is only meaningful when the axes are comparable, and a great many disappointing models are disappointing for exactly this reason.

Why this matters in AI

Before a model can work with an image, a sentence, or a user, it converts that thing into a vector. In the field these are called embeddings. From that point on the model is not looking at pixels or letters at all. It is doing geometry on points in space, and almost everything it does reduces to asking where the points are and how far apart they sit.

Once you have that, several methods you have used stop being separate techniques. k-nearest-neighbours classifies a new point by looking at the known points closest to it. k-means clustering groups points that sit near one another. Anomaly detection runs the same idea backwards and flags the points sitting far away from everything else. All three are the distance you just computed, applied with different intent.

It also explains a piece of advice you have probably followed without being told why. Scaling your features before training is not housekeeping. It is what makes the geometry mean anything.

Next issue takes the obvious next question. Distance asks how far apart two points are, which is the right question for houses and the wrong one for taste, topic, and meaning. There, what matters is whether two vectors point the same way, regardless of how long they are. That is cosine similarity, and it is the formula quietly deciding that two users like the same films and that your query matches a particular document.
