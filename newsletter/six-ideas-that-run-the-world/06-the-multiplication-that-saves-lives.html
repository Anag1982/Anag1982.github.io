Six Ideas · Issue 6

The multiplication that saves lives

30 October 2026·6 min read·Avishek Nag

A drug with a six-hour half-life is taken four times a day. That is not a coincidence or a habit. It is arithmetic, and the arithmetic is the same exponential that grows a tumour and clears a poison. This issue uses one curve to explain a dosing schedule, why a cancer is often years old before it can be found, and why the deadliest diseases are treated with several drugs at once rather than the single best one. The last of these is a multiplication a fifteen-year-old can do, and it saves lives.

The half-life sets the rhythm

A quantity that changes at a rate proportional to how much of it there is grows or decays exponentially; that was the first idea of this whole series. A drug clearing from the blood is the decaying case: the kidneys and liver remove a fixed fraction per hour, not a fixed amount, so the level falls along N(t) = N₀e⁻ᵏᵗ. Ask when the level reaches half its starting value and the starting amount cancels out of the equation entirely, leaving a time that depends only on the clearance rate k: the half-life, t½ = ln 2 / k. Every half-life, whatever is left halves again. It does not matter how much you started with, which is why the half-life, not the dose, sets the rhythm of treatment.

Now read a prescription with that one fact. Six hours after a dose of a six-hour-half-life drug, half remains; wait much longer and the level drops below the amount that does any good. To hold the level in the useful band you top it up about every half-life, which means roughly every six hours: four times a day. A drug with a 24-hour half-life needs one dose a day. The schedule on the label is the logarithm of 2 divided by a clearance rate, rounded to something a patient can live with. And the top-ups do not pile up forever: each new dose adds to a remainder that keeps halving, so the level settles into a steady sawtooth, rising with each dose and sagging between them, held inside the band.

Thirty doublings in the dark

Run the same curve upwards and it turns sinister. A tumour that starts as a single rogue cell grows by doubling, and a lump is usually too small to detect until it reaches about a cubic centimetre of tissue, which holds on the order of a billion cells. How many doublings from one cell to a billion? Thirty, because 2³⁰ is a little over 1.07 billion. So by the time a cancer can be felt or scanned, most of its history is already behind it, and the last doubling alone added as many cells as every doubling before it put together. The curve is slow at the start and overwhelming at the end, which is exactly why it is so often noticed too late. Exponential does not mean fast. It means the rate of growth is proportional to what is already there.

One in a million, twice

Here is the multiplication. Suppose a single cancer cell has about one chance in a million of carrying a mutation that lets it resist a particular drug. In a tumour of a billion cells, that puts roughly a thousand resistant cells in place before treatment even begins. Give the one drug, even the best one: it clears the million-to-one majority, and the thousand survivors regrow the disease. This is why a single drug so often fails, and no improvement in the drug fixes it.

Now give two drugs together, chosen so their resistance mechanisms are independent. The chance a cell resists the first is one in a million. The chance it independently resists the second is also one in a million. To survive the pair, a cell needs both at once, and for independent events the probabilities multiply: one in a million times one in a million is one in a million million, 10⁻¹². In a tumour of 10⁹ cells the expected number of doubly resistant cells is 10⁹ × 10⁻¹², about one in a thousand, meaning only about one such tumour in a thousand starts with even a single cell that can dodge both drugs. The step from a thousand survivors to almost certainly none is bought by one multiplication from school probability.

In the early 1960s that multiplication was a gamble nobody respectable wanted taken. Childhood acute leukemia was a death sentence, and when Emil Frei and Emil Freireich at the US National Cancer Institute proposed hitting it with four toxic drugs at once, colleagues objected that piling poisons on dying children was indefensible. The combination worked. Remissions that single drugs could not hold became durable, and a disease that had been uniformly fatal became one that most children now survive. The reasoning behind the regimen is the arithmetic above, and the same logic now runs the standard treatments for tuberculosis and HIV. It is an uncomfortable story honestly told: the early patients bore real suffering, not all were saved, and the idea was resisted by serious people for serious reasons. That is often how a treatment is born, an argument from probability first, the survival curves later.

One caveat belongs on the page. The single decaying exponential is a first approximation: a real drug moves between blood and tissue, so its fall is two or more linked exponentials, and resistance is not a fixed probability but something that evolves under the drug’s own pressure. Both refinements are university material, pharmacokinetics and population genetics, and neither changes the two results that matter here: the half-life sets the schedule, and the multiplication is why combinations win.

Next issue turns from treating a body to seeing inside one. On 1 October 1971, at a hospital in London, a woman was slid into a machine no doctor had used on a patient before, a computer worked overnight, and the next morning there was a picture of the inside of her living skull. The mathematics that made it possible was not new physics but simultaneous equations, thousands of them, each one an X-ray’s path summed through the body, and the engineer who built the machine never went to university.
