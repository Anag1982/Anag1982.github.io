Six Ideas · Issue 3

The straight line that became AI

18 September 2026·6 min read·Avishek Nag

On the first night of the nineteenth century, an astronomer found a new object between Mars and Jupiter, watched it for six weeks, and lost it in the Sun’s glare. It was the dwarf planet Ceres, and it looked gone for good. A 24-year-old named Carl Friedrich Gauss took the scattered observations, fitted them, and told the astronomers where to look. Ceres reappeared almost exactly where he said. The method he leaned on, fitting a model to imperfect data by making the total error as small as possible, is called least squares, and it is the direct ancestor of the systems now called artificial intelligence. This issue derives it with school calculus, then shows in one picture how the same idea, stacked up, becomes a neural network.

The question

Given a cloud of data points that roughly follow a line, which line is best, and what does best even mean? It is not obvious, because infinitely many lines pass near the data and none passes through all of it. Real measurements carry noise, so demanding a line through every point is demanding the impossible. The way out is to change the question: not the line that hits the points, but the line that misses them by as little as possible.

Make “as little as possible” precise. Model the data with a straight line, y = mx + c. For each data point the line makes an error, the vertical gap between the real y and the line’s prediction. Square each gap, so overshoots and undershoots both count as positive and big misses cost more than small ones, then add them up. The best line is the one that makes that total smallest. The squared gaps are called residuals, and the recipe is least squares.

The derivation, in one move you already own

Call the total squared error E. It depends on the two numbers you get to choose, the slope m and the intercept c. At the lowest point of a valley the ground is flat, so the best m and c are where the rate of change of E is zero in both directions, and that is the derivative from Chapter 1 doing its usual job of finding the bottom.

Differentiate E with respect to c and set the result to zero, and out falls a fact worth keeping: the best line must pass through the average point, the point whose coordinates are the mean of the x values and the mean of the y values. Differentiate with respect to m, substitute the first result, and the slope comes out as a formula you can compute with a pencil: how x and y vary together, divided by how x varies on its own. Two numbers, and the best line is fixed. No new mathematics entered, only the derivative, used to find the floor of a valley.

Try it on five real points: (1, 2.1), (2, 3.9), (3, 6.1), (4, 8.0), (5, 9.9). The averages are 3 and 6. Work the two sums and the slope comes out at about 1.97, the intercept at about 0.09. The data were points near the line y = 2x with small wobbles, and least squares has recovered it. Three lines of code confirm the pencil:

import numpy as np
x = np.array([1, 2, 3, 4, 5.]);  y = np.array([2.1, 3.9, 6.1, 8.0, 9.9])
m = ((x - x.mean()) * (y - y.mean())).sum() / ((x - x.mean())**2).sum()
print(m, y.mean() - m * x.mean())   # 1.97  0.09

The computer is doing the arithmetic you just did, not magic. That sentence is most of what this book wants you to take from the chapter.

The bridge to AI, shorter than it looks

An artificial neuron takes several inputs, multiplies each by a weight, adds them up, and fires if the total passes a threshold. With two inputs, the set of points where it just barely fires is a straight line dividing the plane into fire and do-not-fire. A neuron is a line. Training a neuron is choosing the weights, which is choosing a line, which is the fitting problem you just solved.

One line, though, cannot do everything, and the failure is drawable. Colour the four corners of a square so that opposite corners match, the pattern called XOR. Try to separate the two colours with a single straight line and you will fail, because any line leaves a matched pair split across it. But two lines can fence the middle band off from the corners, and stacking neurons into two layers gives you those two lines and a way to combine them. That single picture is the reason deep networks are deep: one layer draws straight boundaries, and more layers bend and combine them into any shape.

Where school ends

Real problems have many inputs, not one, so the line becomes a plane in many dimensions and the bookkeeping needs matrices and vectors, which is the linear algebra of a first-year engineering course. Real models are often curves and thresholds rather than lines, which needs logistic and nonlinear fitting. And a large network has far too many weights to solve with one formula, so instead of the exact answer above it is trained step by step, walking downhill on the error. The idea you now have, fit a model by making the squared error small, survives all of it. Only the machinery grows.

The person behind it

Least squares is also a lesson in how credit works in science. Adrien-Marie Legendre published the method clearly in 1805, in an appendix on the orbits of comets, and gave it its name. Gauss, in 1809, wrote that he had been using it since 1795 and had relied on it to recover Ceres in 1801. Legendre was furious, and the two never settled it. Both were right in their way: Legendre published first, and Gauss very likely used it first and pushed it further by tying it to probability.

The quarrel matters because it shows the ordinary shape of discovery. A useful idea is often in the air, arrived at independently by more than one person, and the question of who owns it is rarely clean. What survived the argument is the method itself, which now runs quietly inside every fitted line, every calibration, and every trained model. The people are remembered for the fight. The mathematics does not care who won.

Next issue takes the step this one set up. When a model has too many knobs for any formula to set at once, the machine finds them the way you would find the bottom of a valley in thick fog: feel the slope under your feet, take a small step downhill, feel again. That walk was written down by Cauchy in 1847 in a three-page note, it is called gradient descent, and it is the engine that trains every large AI system running today. You will run it by hand and watch one wrong number make it fly apart.
