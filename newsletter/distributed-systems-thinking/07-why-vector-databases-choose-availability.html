The Expensive Answers · Issue 7

Why vector databases choose availability

30 October 2026·4 min read·Avishek Nag

Every AI system with retrieval has, at its heart, a store full of embeddings, and that store has quietly made one of the oldest decisions in distributed systems for you. It is worth knowing which way it decided, and why the decision is right.

The CAP theorem, informally: when a network partition splits your replicated store, you choose between consistency (refuse to serve rather than risk stale answers) and availability (keep serving, reconcile later). Banks choose consistency: an ATM that cannot reach the ledger refuses to dispense, because wrong balances cost more than downtime. The interesting question for an architect is never which choice is better; it is where each piece of state belongs, and the embedding index is the cleanest case study I know.

Retrieval was never exact

Start with what the index actually promises. A nearest-neighbour search over sixty million vectors does not answer “fetch row 42”; it answers “what is most similar to this,” approximately, on purpose. The index structures that make such search fast trade a little exactness for a lot of speed as a design decision. Your retrieval was never exact. That is not a defect; it is the product.

Now run the partition scenario. Replicas split; one is missing the last twenty minutes of document updates. Serve from it? The stale replica returns passages that were the best answer twenty minutes ago, which, for nearly every question ever asked, is indistinguishable from the best answer now, and is in any case within the tolerance the approximate index already established. Refuse to serve? Then retrieval is down, and since every question begins with retrieval, the product is down. Stale is survivable. Unavailable is not.

Derived data can afford relaxed guarantees

There is a third fact that seals the argument: the index is derived data. It can be rebuilt, entirely, from source documents plus an embedding model version. A store whose worst-case loss is “replay the log” can afford relaxed guarantees that a store of irreplaceable facts cannot. Your tenant records and billing rows are sources, and they should sit at the consistent end with strict quorums. Your index is a derivation, and it belongs at the available end, tuned for speed, with a freshness SLO instead of a consistency guarantee.

A freshness SLO, not a consistency pretence

That sentence, freshness SLO instead of consistency guarantee, is the practical takeaway. Promise “documents are searchable within fifteen minutes,” monitor it, and alarm on it, rather than pretending the index is a database of record. Write the placement down: this state, this point on the spectrum, this business consequence of the gap. The half-page table that results is one of the highest-value artifacts an architecture can have, and almost nobody writes it.

One caution to keep the argument honest: availability-tuned does not mean carelessly configured. The index still wants replication for durability of the derived state, still wants its staleness measured, and still wants the rebuild path rehearsed, because a derivation you have never rebuilt is a backup you have never restored.

A question worth putting to your own team this week: what is in your placement table’s most-surprising row, the state you discovered was strongly consistent by accident, or eventual by accident, at cost? Next issue leaves the stores and follows the model itself into production, where an evaluation score of ninety-four percent quietly becomes seventy-one three weeks after launch with every dashboard green. The culprit is almost never the model. It is two implementations of the same feature definition, agreeing on the whiteboard and diverging in the third decimal place, and the fix is the oldest move in this series: treat two computations of the truth as a consistency problem.
