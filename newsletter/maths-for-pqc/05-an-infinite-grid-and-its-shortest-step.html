After Shor · Issue 5

An infinite grid, and its shortest step

16 October 2026·8 min read·Avishek Nag

Four issues of this series built an algebra: clock arithmetic, polynomials on the clock face, and a transform that multiplies them fast. None of that algebra, by itself, explains why recovering a secret key is hard. Nothing built so far is hard to undo. Hardness is a geometric claim, not an algebraic one: every ciphertext, public key and signature in the standards is secretly a point in a lattice, and the reason nobody has ever recovered a secret key from a public one is that finding a short nonzero vector in a lattice of the right size and shape is believed to take longer than anyone has time to wait. This issue builds the object from scratch, because the word “lattice” appears in neither FIPS 203 nor FIPS 204, and it is doing all of their security work.

A grid you can draw

Take two vectors in the plane, b₁ = (4, 2) and b₂ = (1, 3). The lattice they generate is every point you can reach by adding whole-number multiples of them: c₁b₁ + c₂b₂ for integers c₁ and c₂. The result is an infinite grid of isolated points, and that isolation is not a technicality. A lattice is discrete: every point sits at the centre of an empty ball of some fixed radius, and only finitely many lattice points fall in any bounded region. Compare the rationals, which crowd infinitely close to everything. Discreteness is the entire reason “the shortest nonzero vector” is a well-posed question. In a dense set, the infimum of nonzero lengths is zero and no vector achieves it; in a lattice, the shortest nonzero vector genuinely exists, and its length gets a name, λ₁. That one number is what an attacker against a lattice-based scheme is ultimately hunting.

Same dots, many descriptions

A basis is a description of a lattice, not the lattice itself, and the same grid has infinitely many. Apply the row operation “add three times the second row to the first” to our basis and you get b₁′ = (7, 11), b₂′ = (1, 3): a legal basis, generating exactly the same dots, and a much worse description of them, with a first vector of length 13 where the original managed 4.5. The general rule is clean enough to state in full. Two bases generate the same lattice exactly when one is an integer matrix of determinant ±1 times the other, a unimodular transform, because that is precisely the condition under which the change of basis and its inverse both have integer entries. So “how long is the lattice’s basis vector” is not a well-formed question; every lattice has arbitrarily bad bases available. The honest questions are about quantities that do not move when the basis does.

import numpy as np
from itertools import product

B  = np.array([[4, 2], [1, 3]])        # one basis, rows are basis vectors
U  = np.array([[1, 3], [0, 1]])        # unimodular: integer, det = 1
B2 = U @ B
print(B2[0], B2[1])                    # [ 7 11] [1 3] -> a second basis

print(abs(round(np.linalg.det(B))),   # 10
      abs(round(np.linalg.det(B2))))  # 10 -> the determinant cannot hide

best, best_len = None, np.inf          # brute-force the shortest vector
for c1, c2 in product(range(-6, 7), repeat=2):
    if c1 == c2 == 0: continue
    v = c1*B[0] + c2*B[1]
    if np.hypot(*v) < best_len: best, best_len = v, np.hypot(*v)
print(best, round(best_len, 3))        # [-3  1] 3.162
print(round(2*np.sqrt(10/np.pi), 3))   # 3.568 -> Minkowski's bound holds

The first such quantity is the determinant: take any basis, compute the absolute value of its matrix determinant, and every basis of the lattice gives the same number, because the unimodular factor contributes exactly ±1. Both bases above give 10. Geometrically, 10 is the area of the parallelogram the basis vectors span, and translated copies of that parallelogram, one per lattice point, tile the plane with no gaps and no overlaps, so the determinant measures how much area belongs to each point, with the basis nowhere in its definition. A long thin parallelogram and a squat near-square one for the same lattice enclose the same area.

A guarantee and a guess

How short can λ₁ be? Two answers matter, and the discipline of telling them apart is half this issue. The guarantee is Minkowski’s theorem, from the 1890s: any symmetric convex region of volume larger than 2ⁿ times the determinant must contain a nonzero lattice point, and applied to a ball it promises, for our grid, a lattice point within radius 2√(10/π) ≈ 3.568 of the origin, before anyone goes looking. The proof is a pigeonhole argument on the tiling above and holds for every lattice without exception. And indeed the true shortest vector, found by the brute-force loop in the code, is (−3, 1), of length √10 ≈ 3.162, comfortably inside the guaranteed circle.

The guess is the Gaussian heuristic: on average one lattice point per determinant’s worth of volume, so a ball expected to hold one point has radius near √(n/2πe) · det1/n. It is a prediction about typical lattices, built from an averaging argument and an approximation accurate only in large dimension, and it is deliberately not a theorem. On our two-dimensional toy it predicts 1.08 against the true 3.162, wrong by a factor of three, which is not a contradiction but a reminder: a formula built for dimension 256 should not be trusted at dimension 2. The distinction is anything but pedantry. An attacker’s goal against a well-chosen lattice is to find something as short as the heuristic predicts, and a defender’s goal is to pick parameters where even heuristic-typical behaviour costs more than anyone can spend.

The lattices the standards actually use

Every lattice the standards touch is built from a matrix A over Zq in one of two ways, and the first is worth meeting concretely: Λ⊥(A), the set of integer vectors x with Ax ≡ 0 (mod q). A toy makes it visible. Take q = 5 and the single row A = (2, 1), so the condition is 2x₁ + x₂ ≡ 0 (mod 5). The vectors (2, 1) and (−1, 2) both satisfy it, they are independent, and the determinant of the basis they form is 5, exactly q to the power of the number of rows of A, which a counting argument forces in general: the map x → Ax mod q sorts all of Zⁿ into qᵐ equal classes, and the lattice is the class of zero. The grid is a five-times-sparser sublattice of the integer grid. ML-KEM’s key-generation lattice has the same definition with n in the hundreds, and the module structure of issue 3 means its A is filled with ring elements rather than single numbers; the picture does not scale to paper, but the algebra does not change. Finding a short nonzero vector in Λ⊥(A) means finding a small x with Ax ≡ 0, which is the Short Integer Solution problem, and it is precisely the problem ML-DSA’s signatures are built to be as hard as.

Why does any of this resist a quantum computer, when issue 1’s machine broke factoring outright? Shor’s algorithm works because factoring hides a neat periodic structure, a group a quantum Fourier transform can read. Lattice problems have no comparably useful structure: the natural quantum formulation runs through a much more awkward group, the known quantum approaches to it have stayed impractical after three decades of effort, and meanwhile the best classical and quantum attacks on real parameters differ by far less than a Shor-sized gap. None of that is a proof, and this series will not pretend otherwise; it is the honest state of the evidence, and the standards’ parameters are sized against the attacks that actually exist.

One loose end is now visible, and it is the series’ next destination. If the same lattice has good bases and bad ones, then everything turns on which basis you hold: the public key will turn out to be a terrible basis, the secret key a good one, and “how hard is the scheme” becomes “how hard is it to turn bad into good”. Next issue states the hard problems properly, the shortest vector, the closest vector, and their approximation versions, and is precise about what “hard” does and does not promise, because the honest version of that promise is what the migration is actually resting on.
