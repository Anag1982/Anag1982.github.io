After Shor · Issue 4

The transform that splits the ring

2 October 2026·8 min read·Avishek Nag

Last issue ended with a bill and a promise. The bill: one ring product in Rq, computed straight from the convolution formula, costs n² scalar multiplications, 65,536 of them at n = 256, and a matrix-vector product needs kl ring products. The promise: the divisibility check on q, which decided how far xⁿ + 1 splits, is exactly what a fast algorithm exploits. This issue builds the machine that cashes it: the number-theoretic transform, which is the fast Fourier transform wearing modular clothes, derived here from nothing beyond the Chinese Remainder Theorem and the roots last issue already found.

The same trick, one level up

Issue 2’s Chinese Remainder Theorem said that factoring an integer modulus into coprime pieces turns one big arithmetic into independent parallel arithmetics, one per piece. The same theorem holds with polynomials in place of integers. If xⁿ + 1 factors modulo q into pairwise coprime pieces, then Rq splits into a product of small rings, one per factor, and multiplication on the right-hand side is done coordinate by coordinate. Call the splitting map the transform: forward, it sends a polynomial to its tuple of residues; inverse, it reassembles the polynomial. A negacyclic product then becomes transform, multiply pointwise, transform back. Nothing is faster yet, because the transform itself still has to be computed, but the algebraic idea costs nothing new. It is the CRT, run on polynomials.

Evaluate at the odd powers

Take the case last issue called complete: q ≡ 1 (mod 2n), a primitive 2n-th root of unity ψ exists, and xⁿ + 1 splits into n distinct linear factors x − ψ²ⁱ⁺¹. Reducing a polynomial modulo a linear factor x − a is, by the remainder theorem, the same thing as evaluating it at a. So the forward transform is pure evaluation: the i-th coordinate of f̂ is f(ψ²ⁱ⁺¹) mod q, the polynomial read off at the i-th odd power of ψ. The inverse is the CRT reconstruction, and it comes out as the mirror formula, a sum against the negative powers of ψ with one factor of n⁻¹ in front, which exists because q is prime.

Run the toy case by hand, because it makes the whole construction concrete. Last issue found that for q = 17, n = 4, the roots of x⁴ + 1 are {2, 8, 9, 15}, the odd powers of ψ = 9 in the order 9, 15, 8, 2. Take f = 1 + 2x + 3x² + 4x³ and evaluate at each root modulo 17: f̂ = (f(9), f(15), f(8), f(2)) = (16, 11, 13, 15). Fourteen lines verify the round trip and the claim the whole construction exists for:

q, n, psi = 17, 4, 9

def ntt(f):
    return [sum(c * pow(psi, (2*i+1)*j, q) for j, c in enumerate(f)) % q
            for i in range(n)]

def intt(fh):
    ninv = pow(n, -1, q)
    return [ninv * sum(fh[i] * pow(psi, -(2*i+1)*j, q) for i in range(n)) % q
            for j in range(n)]

f, g = [1, 2, 3, 4], [5, 0, 2, 1]
print(ntt(f))                                  # [16, 11, 13, 15]
print(intt(ntt(f)))                            # [1, 2, 3, 4] -> round trip exact
h = [a * b % q for a, b in zip(ntt(f), ntt(g))]
print(intt(h))                                 # [14, 16, 13, 8]
# identical to last issue's negacyclic convolution of f and g

Pointwise products in the transform domain invert back to exactly the negacyclic product, and every quantity in sight is an integer modulo q. That last point deserves a sentence, because a misconception runs the other way. The NTT is not an approximation of the FFT adapted for computers; the relationship is the reverse. The FFT, run on cos and sin, rounds, because its roots of unity are irrational and floating point truncates them. The NTT’s roots of unity live in Zq, so it is exact, with no rounding anywhere, at the cost of a constraint the FFT never faces: not every q contains the roots a given transform length needs, which is precisely what last issue’s divisibility check was checking.

Splitting by square roots

As written above, both the forward and inverse maps still cost n² multiplications, each output computed from scratch. The fast algorithm shares work instead, and the sharing rests on one derivable move. Suppose some ring Zq[x]/(x²ᵐ − Γ) appears in the decomposition, and Γ has a square root δ in Zq. Then x²ᵐ − Γ = (xᵐ − δ)(xᵐ + δ), the two factors are coprime because q is odd, and the CRT applies one level down: the ring splits in half, each child the same shape as the parent. Apply the move again wherever a square root exists.

Track the recursion by exponents. The top ring is x²⁵⁶ − (−1), and in ML-KEM’s Z₃₃₂₉ the element −1 is ζ¹²⁸ for the standard’s root ζ = 17 of order 256. Splitting needs a square root of ζ¹²⁸, which is ζ⁶⁴, and the two children carry exponents 64 and 192. Every split halves the surviving exponent, and every split demands the current exponent be even. Starting from 128 = 2⁷, the halving succeeds exactly seven times, and then every surviving exponent is odd. An odd power of ζ provably has no square root modulo 3329: a square root would be an element of order 512, and last issue’s factorisation 3328 = 2⁸ · 13 showed that no such element exists, since 512 does not divide 3328. So the recursion stops at 128 quadratic factors, not because the algorithm gives up but because the ring has run out of square roots. The bit-reversed index pattern that looks so mysterious in the standard’s pseudocode is nothing but the bookkeeping this binary splitting leaves behind.

Each split costs almost nothing at runtime. One level of the transform is n/2 butterflies, each a single multiplication by a power of ζ followed by one addition and one subtraction, and ML-KEM uses seven levels, so a full forward transform is 7 × 128 = 896 multiplications instead of 65,536. The same butterfly is the FFT taught in every signal-processing course, run over Zq instead of the complex numbers. At the bottom, a pointwise product must multiply two degree-one polynomials modulo x² − γ, which expands to four multiplications and three additions per leaf, the standard’s BaseCaseMultiply, with γ fixed per leaf and public.

And here the two standards part company by exactly one factor of two. ML-DSA’s modulus has q − 1 = 8380416 = 2¹³ · 3 · 11 · 31, divisible by 512, so its root ψ = 1753 has order 2n rather than n, the halving succeeds eight times instead of seven, and the recursion runs all the way to 256 linear factors. Its pointwise multiplication is plain entrywise scalar multiplication, no quadratic leaves needed. Same algorithm, same bookkeeping, one extra split. The two transforms that look so different in the two standards’ pseudocode differ by nothing else.

What it buys

Put the pieces against last issue’s cost table. The module product As at ML-KEM-768’s k = l = 3 costs, naively, nine negacyclic products at 65,536 multiplications each: 589,824. Via the transform: convert the nine entries of A and the three of s, multiply pointwise in nine blocks of 128 leaves at four multiplications each, sum, and transform the three results back, which is fifteen transforms at 896 multiplications plus 4,608 leaf multiplications, a total of 18,048. That is a saving of more than thirtyfold on the complete job, inverse transforms included. The gap widens with n, which is the right-hand panel of the figure. This is why the module structure of last issue and this issue’s transform are inseparable in every real implementation: one supplies the structure, the other makes using it affordable, and together they are the reason a post-quantum key exchange costs microseconds rather than milliseconds.

The arithmetic story of this series is now complete: a clock face, polynomials on it, and a transform that multiplies them fast. What none of it explains is where the security lives, because nothing so far is hard to undo. Next issue starts the second half of the book’s title: the lattice, an infinite grid of points generated by a handful of basis vectors, the innocent-looking problem of finding the shortest nonzero point in the grid, and the reason that problem stays hard even for the quantum computer that broke Chapter 1’s world.
