After Shor · Issue 2

Clock arithmetic, and the one gap in it

4 September 2026·7 min read·Avishek Nag

On the clock on your wall, 12 + 3 = 3, and nobody is troubled. The clock reduces every count to its remainder on dividing by 12, and the arithmetic works: you can add and multiply hours and the answers are consistent. Take that idea seriously, swap the 12 for a prime with a few thousand or a few million on it, and you have the algebraic structure that every operation in the post-quantum standards runs on. Nothing in ML-KEM or ML-DSA computes with anything else.

Last issue promised that the mathematics would start at the very bottom. This is the bottom: the ring of integers mod q, the one place its arithmetic fails, and the repair that is the first genuinely beautiful thing in the subject.

The ring Zq

Fix an integer q ≥ 2. Two integers are congruent mod q when they differ by a multiple of q, and Zq = {0, 1, ..., q − 1} names one representative from each class. Addition and multiplication descend unambiguously: reduce after every operation and the answer matches reducing once at the end. That is all “mod” ever means computationally. FIPS 203, the key-establishment standard, works over Zq with q = 3329. FIPS 204, the signature standard, uses q = 8380417. Both are prime, and that is not a detail, as we will see.

One warning before anything else, because it bites implementers rather than mathematicians. Zq has no preferred representatives: {0, ..., q − 1} and the centred set {−⌊q/2⌋, ..., ⌈q/2⌉ − 1} name the same ring. But ML-KEM’s noise is drawn to be small in absolute value, and “small” is a statement about the centred representative: the unsigned residue q − 1 and the centred residue −1 are the same ring element and utterly different noise magnitudes. Fix a convention and say so. The two silently disagree on every negative number.

The gap: division

Addition, subtraction and multiplication all work in Zq without complaint. Division is the gap. Dividing by a means multiplying by an element a−1 with a · a−1 ≡ 1 (mod q), and such an inverse exists exactly when gcd(a, q) = 1. When it fails, it fails hard: in Z4, 2 × 2 ≡ 0, so 2 multiplied by anything is 0 or 2, and nothing multiplies it to 1. A composite modulus leaves holes in the arithmetic wherever a shared factor sits.

The repair, when gcd(a, q) = 1, is the extended Euclidean algorithm. The ordinary Euclidean algorithm finds the gcd by repeated division with remainder; run the same recursion carrying coefficients along and it terminates in Bézout’s identity, integers s and t with s·a + t·q = gcd(a, q). When the gcd is 1, read that equation mod q: s·a ≡ 1, so s is the inverse, delivered by the same algorithm that proved it exists. Here it is doing real work on the real modulus:

def inverse_euclid(a, q):
    old_r, r = a, q
    old_s, s = 1, 0
    while r != 0:
        k = old_r // r
        old_r, r = r, old_r - k * r
        old_s, s = s, old_s - k * s
    return old_s % q            # valid when gcd(a, q) == 1

print(inverse_euclid(17, 3329))   # 1175
print(17 * 1175 % 3329)           # 1

Now the consequence the standards are built on. When q is prime, every nonzero element is coprime to q, so every nonzero element has an inverse, and Zq is a field: a system where all four operations work everywhere. This is why FIPS 203 and 204 insist on prime q. The scheme needs to divide, everywhere, with no holes, and only a prime buys that.

A second way to invert, and why implementations prefer it

There is a second route to the same inverse, through a theorem of Fermat. For prime p and any a not divisible by p, multiplication by a merely shuffles the nonzero residues, and comparing the product of all of them before and after the shuffle forces ap−1 ≡ 1 (mod p). Multiply both sides by a−1 and the inverse appears as a power: ap−2 ≡ a−1. Inversion becomes exponentiation, done by repeated squaring in about log p multiplications.

print(pow(7, 11, 13))         # 2, and 7 x 2 = 14 = 1 (mod 13)
print(pow(17, 3327, 3329))    # 1175, the same inverse as before

Why keep two algorithms for one job? Because they differ in something subtler than speed. Extended Euclid takes a number of steps, and at each step a branch, that depend on the value of a. Fermat inversion performs a fixed pattern of squarings and multiplications set entirely by the public exponent q − 2, identical for every a. If a is secret, that difference is everything: an attacker with a stopwatch, a power probe, or shared cache access can often read a data-dependent computation’s secrets from its timing, and a computation whose sequence of operations never varies gives such an attacker nothing. This is the constant-time discipline, and it is why the best-known field-inversion code in deployed cryptography computes a−1 by exponentiation rather than by the shorter, faster, data-dependent Euclid. The theme returns in every implementation chapter of this subject; this is its first appearance, and the cleanest.

The gate every modulus must pass

One more idea completes the toolkit. The order of an element a is the least k with ak ≡ 1: the length of the cycle its powers walk before repeating. Orders always divide q − 1, which is Fermat’s theorem sharpened. An element whose order is exactly q − 1 is a primitive root, and its powers visit every nonzero residue exactly once:

An element of order exactly n, a primitive n-th root of unity, can therefore exist only if n divides q − 1. That single divisibility is the gate every fast-transform-friendly modulus must pass, and it was checked before either standard was written. Factor the two: 3329 − 1 = 3328 = 28 · 13, and 8380417 − 1 = 8380416 = 213 · 3 · 11 · 31. Both standards work with polynomials of degree 256. Since 256 divides 3328 but 512 does not, ML-KEM’s modulus has 256-th roots of unity and no 512-th roots; ML-DSA’s has both. That one arithmetic fact is why the two standards’ fast transforms will look slightly different when we build them, one complete and one stopping a level early. Choosing q is choosing which transforms are even possible, before a single coefficient is multiplied. And note the trap in the neighbouring claim: primality makes Zq a field, but it says nothing about which roots of unity live inside it. Both conditions were engineered into these moduli separately.

That is the ground floor built: a clock face where all four operations work, two ways to divide with one of them safe to run on secrets, and a divisibility test that decides what the modulus can support. Next issue we glue polynomials onto it. ML-KEM does not live in Zq itself but in the ring Rq = Zq[x]/(x²⁵⁶ + 1), polynomials of degree under 256 whose coefficients are clock-face numbers, and the strange-looking divisor x²⁵⁶ + 1 turns out to be one of the best engineering decisions in the whole standard.
